\section{Discussion}

This study isolates a constraint on rapid associative learning: information
stored in a population state must remain accessible to the circuit that reads
it. A fixed permutation preserved the values of the instructed CA1 vector
but impaired reconstruction by an unchanged decoder. Transforming the decoder
with the same permutation restored performance. This rescue follows from the
model's coordinate symmetry, rather than providing an independent empirical
discovery. Its value is to separate an incompatible readout from degradation
of the target representation. The additional track, degradation, and load
assays show how associative learning operates within the compatible basis,
and where its performance fails.

The contribution is therefore not the introduction of structured hippocampal
representations or of a learning role for CA1. Earlier models assign the
direct EC--CA1 pathway a role in learning shared statistical structure
\citep{schapiro2017}, and scaffold-based theories use pre-existing retrieval
states to organize new associations \citep{chandra2025}. Our model makes a
related but narrower distinction between retrieval-key construction,
CA3--CA1 association, and output decoding. It removes recurrent retrieval
dynamics to expose the relation between the latter two operations. This
simplification allows a controlled compatibility assay, but precludes
claims that the architecture accounts for the broader episodic or spatial
capacities of those models.

Pretraining also sets the scope of generalization. In the track assays the
autoencoder was exposed to the same generated lap banks later used for
associative storage. Novelty therefore resides in the CA3--CA1 associations,
not in learning an unseen input distribution. The circuit additionally omits
dentate gyrus, explicit inhibition, and consolidation; these omissions make it
an assay of the proposed constraint rather than a comprehensive hippocampal
model.

Compatibility also differs from representational invariance. A code need
not remain unchanged if its variations preserve the output required by a
task, or if downstream weights adapt appropriately. Motor-cortical studies
demonstrate constraints imposed by an existing activity repertoire
\citep{sadtler2014,golub2018}; stable latent dynamics further distinguish
population-level structure from fixed decoding of individual recorded
neurons \citep{gallego2020}. Models of self-healing readouts offer a
complementary solution in which downstream plasticity tracks changes in the
input code \citep{rule2022}. Here the decoder is deliberately frozen.
Accordingly, the simulations establish the cost of a mismatch under a fixed
readout, not the necessity of preserving CA1 coordinates when recipient
circuits are free to adapt. Long-term stability and representational drift
remain outside the tested regime.

The two plasticity rules implement different computational uses of an
instruction. The instructive-driven rule updates an association without
comparing the target with current recall; the error-driven rule corrects
under- and overexpressed CA1 components through bounded synaptic changes.
Both yielded similar aligned one-shot reconstruction under the reference
configuration, but repeated error-driven learning produced higher clean
position accuracy and greater cross-context spatial stability. These results
show that matching the target basis does not guarantee identical learning
dynamics or equally discriminable outputs. Although motivated by rapid
instructed plasticity \citep{bittner2017,milstein2021,wu2025}, neither rule
implements a temporal eligibility trace or a dendritic plateau. In
particular, the signed target--recall discrepancy in the error-driven rule
is a computational assumption, not an experimentally identified CA1 error
signal. Evidence for entorhinal teaching signals and nonspatial BTSP-like
field formation supports the motif, but not this exact implementation
\citep{grienberger2022,dorian2026a}.

The cue-track results reveal reproducible context dependence rather than
unconstrained creation of a new map at each transition. Population maps were
similar within a cue assignment and when that assignment recurred.
Consecutive-lap tuning similarity fell at exchanges and recovered on the
following lap, while individual units showed continuous mixtures of spatial
stability and cue modulation. These features are qualitatively related to
cue-sensitive changes in hippocampal firing \citep{muller1987,leutgeb2005}
and to learning-related CA1 field reorganization
\citep{grienberger2022,madar2025,vaidya2025}. However, the transition assay
changes both the training context and the context used for the recall probe.
The boundary discontinuity therefore combines changes in the learned weights
with the immediate effect of presenting a different retrieval key. Pausing
plasticity during each probe prevents further learning during measurement;
it does not isolate the contribution of plasticity to the transition.
Fixed-context probes across training, together with context switches at fixed
weights, would be needed to distinguish learning-driven reorganization from
cue-dependent re-expression. Nor were the simulated unit distributions fitted
to biological field statistics or used to classify rate and global remapping.

The degradation experiments likewise support a specific interpretation.
Partial input loss left useful output information under the normal key map,
and corruption of one input partition preferentially affected its associated
readout. Yet there is no recurrent CA3 network or attractor settling:
robustness arises from a distributed feedforward input-to-key transformation
and the learned key-to-content mapping. Degraded-input reconstruction alone
cannot identify a recurrent completion mechanism
\citep{nakazawa2002,li2024}. The dense common-key control makes every CA3-like
unit receive the same EC average and eliminates item-specific keys. Its
failure establishes the importance of distinguishable keys in this circuit,
not a unique advantage of sparse connectivity. Sparsity-matched and
dimensionality-matched controls would be required for that stronger claim.

The constructed MEC/LEC partition also limits anatomical interpretation.
Each input half has a designated information type and corresponding readout,
so part of the observed dissociation is expected by design. Biological medial
and lateral EC contain mixed signals, and both can contribute to nonspatial
CA1 learning \citep{henriksen2010,deshmukh2011,dorian2026a}. Moreover, the
retrieval query and reconstruction target are derived from the same EC
pattern. Removing input units tests recovery of missing components of a
previously experienced pattern; it does not test retrieval of an independently
specified episode feature absent from the query. Such a heteroassociative
task would provide a stronger test of cue-to-content retrieval.

The optimization-informed analyses extend, but do not replace, the matched
rule comparison. Separate searches selected markedly different fan-in,
sparsity, gain, and learning-rate combinations. Their sensitivity and load
curves therefore compare complete operating regimes, not update equations
in isolation. Held-out perturbations indicate that useful performance extends
beyond one exact parameter vector, while large changes can be detrimental.
One-at-a-time perturbations do not establish global robustness or identify
unique biological parameters. Similarly, the 90-pair load assay measures an
empirical interference profile. The autoencoder already represents the full
cue vocabulary, total training increases with load, and the 10-cue templates
are sparser than those in the two-cue assay. Exact-pair reconstruction should
not be interpreted as evidence for learning temporal associations between
the cues or as a universal storage-capacity estimate.

Several tests follow directly from these limitations. First, varying target
coordinates and decoder adaptation independently could compare preservation
of a fixed readout with compensatory downstream learning. Second, crossing
probe context with learning history would separate immediate key-dependent
responses from lasting changes in CA1 weights. Third, load experiments that
hold total writes, cue coverage, and template sparsity fixed would clarify
the sources of interference. More broadly, biological tests should compare
the behavioral consequences of CA1 changes with their effects on recipient
populations, rather than treating the magnitude of CA1 change alone as a
measure of memory disruption.

Within these bounds, the model provides a compact demonstration of how a
previously established representational basis can support rapid associative
storage. Its central implication is relational: the usefulness of a retrieved
CA1 state depends on the mapping that interprets it. Readout compatibility is
thus a testable constraint on models of hippocampal learning, while its
biological implementation and long-term maintenance remain open questions.
