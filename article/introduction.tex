\section{Introduction}

Episodic memory requires a circuit to distinguish related experiences, retrieve
stored content from a partial cue, and express that content in a form that
other circuits can use. Hippocampal-indexing and complementary-learning-systems
theories explain how rapid hippocampal storage can complement slower cortical
learning \citep{teyler1986,mcclelland1995}. Within this framework, pattern
separation limits interference and pattern completion supports retrieval.
Here we focus on a complementary requirement: compatibility between a
retrieved population state and the downstream mapping that interprets it.
A representation may preserve information while changing the correspondence
between neural activity and its readout. Successful retrieval therefore
depends not only on what is stored, but also on the coordinates in which it
is expressed.

The hippocampal circuit provides a useful setting in which to examine this
requirement. Entorhinal cortex (EC) reaches CA1 through both the trisynaptic
pathway, via dentate gyrus and CA3, and the direct temporoammonic projection.
Autoassociative theories assign recurrent CA3 circuitry a central role in
content-addressable retrieval \citep{marr1971}, consistent with impaired
partial-cue recall after selective disruption of CA3 NMDA-receptor function
\citep{nakazawa2002}. CA3 and CA1 also exhibit distinct population responses
to environmental changes \citep{leutgeb2004}. Computational accounts already
give CA1 and its direct entorhinal input roles beyond relaying CA3 output:
in a complementary-learning-systems model within the hippocampus, the
monosynaptic pathway learns statistical structure while the trisynaptic
pathway supports memory for individual episodes \citep{schapiro2017}.
More recently, scaffold-based models have separated content storage from the
dynamics that generate stable retrieval states \citep{chandra2025}.
Our question is narrower than either account: given an established output
mapping, how does the compatibility of a newly instructed CA1 state affect
its subsequent reconstruction?

Rapid instructed plasticity provides a candidate computational motif.
Behavioral-timescale synaptic plasticity (BTSP) can create a CA1 place field
after a single dendritic plateau event and bidirectionally reshape existing
fields \citep{bittner2017,milstein2021}. EC layer 3 activity can provide
teaching-related input for learning-dependent CA1 reorganization
\citep{grienberger2022}. Experiments and models link BTSP-like dynamics to
trial-by-trial field shifts and stabilization of task-relevant spatial codes
\citep{madar2025,vaidya2025}, while computational work shows how BTSP-inspired
rules can support rapid content-addressable storage \citep{wu2025}.
Recent evidence also extends the empirical motivation beyond spatial tasks:
large calcium events consistent with BTSP accompany, and experimentally
induced events can drive, rapid formation of odor responses in CA1
\citep{dorian2026a}. These findings motivate an EC-related instruction that
modifies CA3--CA1 synapses. They do not establish that a biological instructive
signal specifies the complete population target used in our model.

The proposed constraint also relates to work on neural population geometry
and readout stability. In motor-cortical brain--computer interfaces,
perturbations within an existing neural manifold are learned more readily
than perturbations requiring activity outside it \citep{sadtler2014};
short-term adaptation can also reassign an established activity repertoire
to different outputs \citep{golub2018}. Stable latent dynamics can support
cross-day behavioral decoding even when fixed decoders applied directly to
recorded neurons deteriorate \citep{gallego2020}. Such latent stability should
not be equated with stability of a neuron-by-neuron readout. Conversely,
models of adaptive readouts show how downstream plasticity can compensate
for an evolving population code \citep{rule2022}. We use \emph{readout
compatibility} as a functional criterion: a recalled CA1 state is compatible
to the extent that an established decoder reconstructs its intended content.
This criterion neither requires every neuron to retain its tuning nor
identifies compatibility with membership of a particular neural manifold.

To make this criterion explicit, we pretrain an EC--CA1--EC autoencoder and
then freeze its encoder and decoder. Plastic CA3--CA1 synapses subsequently
associate a feedforward CA3-like retrieval key with the encoder-defined CA1
target. The pretrained basis abstracts structure accumulated before the
associative learning assay; it is not a proposed biological backpropagation
mechanism. Prior theoretical work motivates using established structure to
support new associations \citep{schapiro2017,chandra2025}, and pretraining
offers an engineering analogue of feature reuse \citep{yosinski2014}.
Freezing the decoder prevents downstream adaptation from compensating for
changes in the instructed coordinates. The model thus tests the consequences
of compatibility under controlled conditions, rather than explaining how
biological circuits acquire or maintain it.

We compare an instructive-driven update, which depends on the target but not
the recalled CA1 state, with a bounded error-driven update that corrects the
target--recall discrepancy. First, a coordinate-permutation assay separates
information preserved in an instructed vector from its accessibility to a
fixed decoder. Second, a cue-guided track assay tests context-dependent CA1
reconfiguration. Third, selective input degradation tests reconstruction
when spatial or cue information is reduced. For these manipulations, EC is
partitioned into MEC-like spatial and LEC-like cue inputs. This separation is
experimental bookkeeping, not an anatomical claim: both medial and lateral
EC contain mixtures of spatial and nonspatial signals
\citep{hargreaves2005,henriksen2010,deshmukh2011,dorian2026a}.
Finally, rule-specific parameter searches, held-out sensitivity analyses,
and an ordered-cue-pair load assay assess how the results depend on the
operating regime. Together, these tests evaluate a representational constraint
on rapid associative learning without attributing recurrent dynamics or
long-term stability to this minimal circuit.
