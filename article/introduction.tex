\section{Introduction}

The hippocampus is commonly described as a fast-learning system that binds
elements of an experience and later retrieves them from partial input.
Hippocampal indexing and complementary-learning-systems theories formalize this
role as rapid episodic storage operating alongside slower cortical learning
\citep{teyler1986,mcclelland1995}. An effective memory system must therefore
separate related episodes, select an appropriate stored state, and make the
retrieved content usable to downstream circuits. The first two requirements are
central to classical theories of pattern separation and completion. Here we
focus on the third: the representational coordinates in which retrieved content
is expressed. This motivates a view of CA1 not merely as a relay or container of
individual memories, but as an adaptive interface that organizes successive
experiences within a structured neural space.

The anatomy of the hippocampal formation suggests a distribution of
computational roles across its subfields. Entorhinal cortex (EC) provides major
input to the hippocampus. Medial EC (MEC) is strongly associated with spatial
variables, including grid-cell and position-related activity, whereas lateral
EC (LEC) more prominently represents objects, sensory features, and salient
events, although the division is neither absolute nor anatomically strict
\citep{hargreaves2005,henriksen2010,deshmukh2011}. The canonical trisynaptic
pathway projects through dentate gyrus to recurrent CA3 circuitry and then,
through Schaffer collaterals, to CA1; CA1 also receives direct EC input through
the temporoammonic pathway. Autoassociative theories assign CA3 a central role
in content-addressable retrieval \citep{marr1971}. Consistent with this view,
selective disruption of CA3 NMDA-receptor function impairs recall from partial
cues, and CA3 and CA1 ensembles exhibit distinct responses to environmental
change \citep{nakazawa2002,leutgeb2004}. More recent models develop recurrent
CA3 dynamics and spatially organized associative scaffolds
\citep{li2024,chandra2025}. These accounts explain how a memory may be selected,
but do not by themselves determine how the selected content should be expressed
at hippocampal output.

CA1 representations can form and reorganize rapidly. Behavioral-timescale
synaptic plasticity (BTSP) can create a place field after a single dendritic
plateau event and can bidirectionally reshape an existing field according to
its relation to the instructive event \citep{bittner2017,milstein2021}. EC layer
3 activity can provide an instructive signal for learning-related changes in
CA1 population activity \citep{grienberger2022}. Recent experiments and models
further connect BTSP-like dynamics to trial-by-trial field shifts and the
progressive stabilization of task-relevant spatial codes
\citep{madar2025,vaidya2025}. BTSP is therefore relevant not only to inserting
an isolated place field, but also to updating a cognitive map as cues and task
contingencies change. Computational work additionally shows that BTSP-like
rules can support rapid content-addressable storage \citep{wu2025}. The present
study abstracts this broad motif of an EC-related instruction acting on
CA3--CA1 synapses; it does not model the temporal eligibility trace, plateau
potential, or molecular mechanisms of biological BTSP.

CA1 activity is also context sensitive. Changes in environmental or cue
configuration can alter firing rates, field positions, or the active
population, producing phenomena commonly described as rate and global
remapping \citep{muller1987,leutgeb2005}. Direct entorhinal input offers a route
by which spatial and nonspatial variables can jointly shape these changes. In
the model below, separate MEC-like and LEC-like input partitions provide a
controlled way to manipulate those variables. This is a modeling abstraction
used to test pathway-selective perturbations, not a claim that biological MEC
and LEC carry pure or independent information channels.

Our central proposal adds a representational constraint to this circuit motif.
If rapid plasticity writes an arbitrary CA1 pattern while its output pathway
remains fixed, the pattern may retain information about an episode yet be
functionally unreadable because its coordinates no longer have the meaning
expected by the recipient circuit. Related constraints have been demonstrated
outside the hippocampus. In brain--computer-interface experiments, motor
cortical perturbations that remained within a pre-existing neural manifold were
learned more readily than perturbations requiring activity outside that
manifold \citep{sadtler2014}; short-term adaptation could also occur by
reassociating an established activity repertoire with new outputs
\citep{golub2018}. Stable latent population dynamics can support behavioral
decoding despite turnover of individual recorded neurons \citep{gallego2020}.
We use the narrower term \emph{readout compatibility} for the requirement that
a newly instructed CA1 state remain expressed in coordinates that an
established output pathway can interpret.

To instantiate prior structure, an EC--CA1--EC autoencoder is pretrained before
new associations are stored. This is a computational device, not a claim that
CA1 is biologically optimized by backpropagation in a separate phase.
Biologically, it abstracts structure supplied by development, accumulated
experience, and relatively stable entorhinal--hippocampal connectivity
\citep{mcclelland1995,chandra2025,wen2024}. In deep learning, pretraining plays
an analogous engineering role by establishing reusable features before rapid
adaptation, although the mechanisms and substrates differ
\citep{yosinski2014}. Freezing the decoder after pretraining turns compatibility
into an explicit assay: a new CA1 state must remain legible to the established
readout rather than being rescued by encoder--decoder co-adaptation. Such a
relation could, in principle, preserve the decodability of successive
experiences over longer timescales, but duration itself is not tested here.

We study this idea in a deliberately minimal model. EC input generates both a
discriminable CA3-like retrieval key and a pretrained instructed CA1 state.
Plastic CA3--CA1 synapses associate the key with that state, and a frozen
CA1--EC decoder reads out recall. We compare an instructive-driven rule
with a bounded error-driven rule that corrects the discrepancy between
instructed and recalled CA1 activity. The simulations ask whether reconstruction
depends on coordinate compatibility, whether cue-sequence changes induce
reproducible CA1 reconfiguration, how the two update rules differ under matched
conditions and near separately selected operating points, and how recall
changes when spatial or cue-like EC input is selectively degraded. A final
load test asks how exact recall changes as more ordered cue pairs are stored.
The resulting claims are constructive and conditional:
the model proposes a representational principle and exposes its consequences,
but does not establish that biological CA3 or CA1 implements the exact
architecture, pretraining procedure, or synaptic rules used here.
