\section*{Appendix A: Evolutionary hyperparameter selection}

\subsection*{Optimization strategy}

Hyperparameters were selected with the covariance matrix adaptation evolution
strategy (CMA-ES), a stochastic, derivative-free optimizer for nonlinear and
non-convex objectives \citep{hansen2016}. CMA-ES sampled a multivariate normal
population in standardized coordinates and adapted its mean, covariance, and
global step size from candidate rankings. If $g$ is a standardized genome,
model parameters were decoded as
\begin{equation}
\theta(g)=\operatorname{clip}_{[\ell,u]}(c+s\odot g),
\label{eq:cma-decoding}
\end{equation}
where $c$, $s$, $\ell$, and $u$ are the parameter-wise centers, scales, lower
bounds, and upper bounds. Count-valued parameters were rounded to the nearest
integer after clipping. A quadratic penalty discouraged samples outside the
standardized bounds before clipping. Candidate evaluations were parallelized
across CPU processes, but parallelization did not change the objective.

The search was used only for engineering model selection. Model weights were
not encoded in the genome: autoencoder weights were still learned with Adam,
and CA3--CA1 weights were still changed by the local plasticity rule. Fixed
development seeds made candidate evaluations deterministic and comparable.
No seed was optimized separately, and development seeds were disjoint from the
20 seeds used for the matched principal experiments, the 10 independent seeds
used for sensitivity analysis, and the 10 seeds used for the cue-pair load
test. A single CMA-ES run was performed for each reported
search, so the results characterize the selected run rather than variability
across independent optimizer restarts.

\subsection*{Autoencoder objective}

The autoencoder search varied $K_{\mathrm{CA1}}$, the latent sparse-activation
gain $\beta_{\mathrm{latent}}$, and the decoder gain
$\beta_{\mathrm{out}}$. It used three development seeds (41001--41003). For
each candidate and seed, separate autoencoders were trained for 64 epochs on
(i) 1,024 sparse binary EC patterns and (ii) 20 MEC+LEC track laps, then tested
on 128 disjoint sparse patterns and five independently generated track laps.
The minimized objective was
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{AE}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{s=1}^{3}
\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sparse},s}(\theta)
+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{track},s}(\theta)\right],
\label{eq:ae-evolution-fitness}
\end{equation}
where each $\mathcal{L}$ is mean squared validation reconstruction error. The
search used 96 generations of seven candidates (672 candidate evaluations)
with boundary-penalty coefficient 0.1. The selected values were
$K_{\mathrm{CA1}}=5$, $\beta_{\mathrm{latent}}=3.912370$, and
$\beta_{\mathrm{out}}=25.333004$. Their mean sparse, track, and equally weighted
validation MSEs were 0.001770, 0.000593, and 0.001182, respectively.

\subsection*{Medial-temporal-lobe (MTL) recall objective}

The autoencoder search above documents an alternative basis optimization. For
the final MTL searches and all figure-generating simulations, however, we
retained the common reference basis ($K_{\mathrm{CA1}}=5$,
$\beta_{\mathrm{latent}}=\beta_{\mathrm{out}}=25$), avoiding a simultaneous
change of representational basis and associative rule. Five
MTL parameters were then optimized independently for the instructive-driven
and error-driven rules: EC--CA3 fan-in $F$, CA3 target activity
$K_{\mathrm{CA3}}$, CA3 and CA1 inverse-temperature gains, and plasticity rate
$\alpha$. For each of three development seeds (42001--42003), one autoencoder,
one bank of clean training and validation laps, and fixed MEC/LEC corruption
masks were prepared before optimization and reused for every candidate and
both rules. Storage vectors were shuffled within each generated lap, with cue
locations carried along with their samples. This preserved the marginal
MEC+LEC distribution while removing sequential adjacency and traversal order.

After 20 clean storage laps, three components were calculated. Clean recall was
the mean of full-output cosine, exact-position accuracy, and cue-identity
accuracy,
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{clean}}=\frac{1}{3}
(C_{\mathrm{EC}}+A_{\mathrm{pos}}+A_{\mathrm{cue}}).
\end{equation}
At 85\% MEC dropout, the score averaged position and cue accuracy; at 85\% LEC
dropout, it averaged cue accuracy and MEC output cosine:
\begin{align}
J_{\mathrm{MECdrop}}&=\frac{1}{2}
(A_{\mathrm{pos}}^{\mathrm{MECdrop}}+
 A_{\mathrm{cue}}^{\mathrm{MECdrop}}),\\
J_{\mathrm{LECdrop}}&=\frac{1}{2}
(A_{\mathrm{cue}}^{\mathrm{LECdrop}}+
 C_{\mathrm{MEC}}^{\mathrm{LECdrop}}).
\end{align}
Each degraded component was averaged over six fixed masks per modality. CMA-ES
maximized the equally weighted mean over components and development seeds,
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{MTL}}(\theta,r)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{s=1}^{3}
\frac{J_{\mathrm{clean},s}+J_{\mathrm{MECdrop},s}+
J_{\mathrm{LECdrop},s}}{3},
\label{eq:mtl-evolution-fitness}
\end{equation}
for rule $r$. Each rule used 96 generations of eight candidates (768 candidate
evaluations) and boundary-penalty coefficient 0.05.

The two searches converged to quantitatively different retrieval regimes. The
instructive-driven solution used broader EC--CA3 fan-in and a sharper CA3
activation but a softer CA1 recall nonlinearity; the error-driven solution used
narrower fan-in, lower CA3 gain, steeper CA1 recall, and a larger learning
rate. These values should not be interpreted as biological estimates or as
proof of global optimality. They indicate that, under the stated objective and
bounds, the two update rules favor different computational regimes. The local multiplier analysis in
Fig.~\ref{fig:degradation}E probes the immediate neighborhood of these selected
vectors; Eq.~\ref{eq:local-sensitivity} and its limitations are described in
Methods.
