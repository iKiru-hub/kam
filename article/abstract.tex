\begin{abstract}
Rapid memory formation requires retrieved content to remain interpretable by
downstream circuits. We examine this requirement, termed \emph{readout
compatibility}, in a minimal hippocampal model. Entorhinal cortex (EC) input
generates a CA3-like retrieval key and a pretrained CA1 target; plastic
CA3--CA1 synapses associate the two, and a fixed CA1--EC decoder reconstructs
the input. Across 20 paired initializations, permuting the target's CA1
coordinates impaired one-shot reconstruction under both an instructive-driven
rule (mean output--target cosine: 0.880 aligned, 0.247 permuted) and a bounded
error-driven rule (0.872, 0.178). Correspondingly permuting the decoder
restored performance, isolating a coordinate mismatch rather than loss of
information from the target. On a circular track, exchanging cue identities
produced reproducible changes in recalled CA1 tuning and alternating
population states associated with cue assignment. Both rules generated
heterogeneous spatially stable and cue-modulated responses. During frozen
recall, selective lateral or medial EC degradation preferentially impaired
cue or position readout, respectively; a common-key control that eliminated
item-specific CA3 activity failed even with intact inputs. Separate parameter
searches identified distinct operating regimes, and held-out sensitivity and
load analyses revealed parameter dependence and interference across up to 90
ordered cue pairs. These simulations show how rapid associative learning can
express retrieved content in coordinates compatible with an established
readout. They demonstrate cue-dependent reconfiguration and degraded-input
robustness in a feedforward architecture, not recurrent CA3 pattern completion,
a biological mechanism for maintaining compatibility, or long-term memory
stability.
\end{abstract}
