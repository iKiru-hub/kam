    Readout-compatible CA1 plasticity supports cue-dependent
 reconfiguration and degraded-cue recall in a minimal hippocampal
                              model

                                             Author Name


                                                 Abstract

         Rapid memory formation requires retrieved content to remain interpretable by downstream
     circuits. We examine this requirement, termed readout compatibility, in a minimal hippocampal
     model. Entorhinal cortex (EC) input generates a CA3-like retrieval key and a pretrained CA1
     target; plastic CA3–CA1 synapses associate the two, and a fixed CA1–EC decoder reconstructs
     the input. Across 20 paired initializations, permuting the target’s CA1 coordinates impaired
     one-shot reconstruction under both an instructive-driven rule (mean output–target cosine: 0.880
     aligned, 0.247 permuted) and a bounded error-driven rule (0.872, 0.178). Correspondingly
     permuting the decoder restored performance, isolating a coordinate mismatch rather than loss of
     information from the target. On a circular track, exchanging cue identities produced reproducible
     changes in recalled CA1 tuning and alternating population states associated with cue assignment.
     Both rules generated heterogeneous spatially stable and cue-modulated responses. During frozen
     recall, selective lateral or medial EC degradation preferentially impaired cue or position readout,
     respectively; a common-key control that eliminated item-specific CA3 activity failed even with
     intact inputs. Separate parameter searches identified distinct operating regimes, and held-out
     sensitivity and load analyses revealed parameter dependence and interference across up to 90
     ordered cue pairs. These simulations show how rapid associative learning can express retrieved
     content in coordinates compatible with an established readout. They demonstrate cue-dependent
     reconfiguration and degraded-input robustness in a feedforward architecture, not recurrent CA3
     pattern completion, a biological mechanism for maintaining compatibility, or long-term memory
     stability.



1    Introduction

Episodic memory requires a circuit to distinguish related experiences, retrieve stored content
from a partial cue, and express that content in a form that other circuits can use. Hippocampal-
indexing and complementary-learning-systems theories explain how rapid hippocampal storage
can complement slower cortical learning [16, 23]. Within this framework, pattern separation
limits interference and pattern completion supports retrieval. Here we focus on a complementary
requirement: compatibility between a retrieved population state and the downstream mapping that
interprets it. A representation may preserve information while changing the correspondence between
neural activity and its readout. Successful retrieval therefore depends not only on what is stored,
but also on the coordinates in which it is expressed.
The hippocampal circuit provides a useful setting in which to examine this requirement. Entorhinal
cortex (EC) reaches CA1 through both the trisynaptic pathway, via dentate gyrus and CA3, and the


                                                     1
direct temporoammonic projection. Autoassociative theories assign recurrent CA3 circuitry a central
role in content-addressable retrieval [15], consistent with impaired partial-cue recall after selective
disruption of CA3 NMDA-receptor function [19]. CA3 and CA1 also exhibit distinct population
responses to environmental changes [11]. Computational accounts already give CA1 and its direct
entorhinal input roles beyond relaying CA3 output: in a complementary-learning-systems model
within the hippocampus, the monosynaptic pathway learns statistical structure while the trisynaptic
pathway supports memory for individual episodes [22]. More recently, scaffold-based models have
separated content storage from the dynamics that generate stable retrieval states [2]. Our question
is narrower than either account: given an established output mapping, how does the compatibility
of a newly instructed CA1 state affect its subsequent reconstruction?
Rapid instructed plasticity provides a candidate computational motif. Behavioral-timescale synaptic
plasticity (BTSP) can create a CA1 place field after a single dendritic plateau event and bidirectionally
reshape existing fields [1, 17]. EC layer 3 activity can provide teaching-related input for learning-
dependent CA1 reorganization [7]. Experiments and models link BTSP-like dynamics to trial-by-trial
field shifts and stabilization of task-relevant spatial codes [14, 24], while computational work shows
how BTSP-inspired rules can support rapid content-addressable storage [25]. Recent evidence also
extends the empirical motivation beyond spatial tasks: large calcium events consistent with BTSP
accompany, and experimentally induced events can drive, rapid formation of odor responses in CA1
[4]. These findings motivate an EC-related instruction that modifies CA3–CA1 synapses. They do
not establish that a biological instructive signal specifies the complete population target used in our
model.
The proposed constraint also relates to work on neural population geometry and readout stability.
In motor-cortical brain–computer interfaces, perturbations within an existing neural manifold are
learned more readily than perturbations requiring activity outside it [21]; short-term adaptation can
also reassign an established activity repertoire to different outputs [6]. Stable latent dynamics can
support cross-day behavioral decoding even when fixed decoders applied directly to recorded neurons
deteriorate [5]. Such latent stability should not be equated with stability of a neuron-by-neuron
readout. Conversely, models of adaptive readouts show how downstream plasticity can compensate
for an evolving population code [20]. We use readout compatibility as a functional criterion: a
recalled CA1 state is compatible to the extent that an established decoder reconstructs its intended
content. This criterion neither requires every neuron to retain its tuning nor identifies compatibility
with membership of a particular neural manifold.
To make this criterion explicit, we pretrain an EC–CA1–EC autoencoder and then freeze its encoder
and decoder. Plastic CA3–CA1 synapses subsequently associate a feedforward CA3-like retrieval
key with the encoder-defined CA1 target. The pretrained basis abstracts structure accumulated
before the associative learning assay; it is not a proposed biological backpropagation mechanism.
Prior theoretical work motivates using established structure to support new associations [2, 22],
and pretraining offers an engineering analogue of feature reuse [26]. Freezing the decoder prevents
downstream adaptation from compensating for changes in the instructed coordinates. The model
thus tests the consequences of compatibility under controlled conditions, rather than explaining
how biological circuits acquire or maintain it.
We compare an instructive-driven update, which depends on the target but not the recalled CA1 state,
with a bounded error-driven update that corrects the target–recall discrepancy. First, a coordinate-
permutation assay separates information preserved in an instructed vector from its accessibility to
a fixed decoder. Second, a cue-guided track assay tests context-dependent CA1 reconfiguration.


                                                   2
Third, selective input degradation tests reconstruction when spatial or cue information is reduced.
For these manipulations, EC is partitioned into MEC-like spatial and LEC-like cue inputs. This
separation is experimental bookkeeping, not an anatomical claim: both medial and lateral EC
contain mixtures of spatial and nonspatial signals [3, 4, 9, 10]. Finally, rule-specific parameter
searches, held-out sensitivity analyses, and an ordered-cue-pair load assay assess how the results
depend on the operating regime. Together, these tests evaluate a representational constraint on
rapid associative learning without attributing recurrent dynamics or long-term stability to this
minimal circuit.


2     Results

2.1   A minimal retrieval-key and instructed-CA1 architecture

The model contains four functional components (Fig. 1A). Entorhinal input, denoted xEC , projects
through a fixed sparse map to a CA3-like population that provides a retrieval key. A separately
pretrained EC–CA1 encoder transforms the same input into an instructed CA1 state (IS). Plastic
CA3–CA1 weights associate the retrieval key with the IS, whereas the corresponding pretrained CA1–
EC decoder remains fixed. During recall, the EC input regenerates a key, the learned association
reconstructs a CA1 state, and the decoder maps that state back to EC output. This architecture
separates the selection of associative content from its expression in a pre-existing output basis.
We first evaluated two plasticity rules under matched inputs and a shared reference parameter
configuration (Fig. 1B). The instructive-driven rule writes an outer-product association between
the instructed CA1 state and the CA3-like key without considering the current recalled state.
The bounded error-driven rule instead potentiates CA3–CA1 synapses for underexpressed CA1
components and depresses them for overexpressed components. Repeated presentations therefore
rewrite the instructed association under the instructive-driven rule but correct a residual under the
error-driven rule. Both are abstract models of rapid instructed plasticity; neither implements the
temporal eligibility trace or dendritic plateau dynamics of biological BTSP.
Track inputs combined a MEC-like spatial component with a LEC-like cue component (Fig. 1D).
Two cue identities occurred at fixed positions and could exchange locations across blocks. For
degradation tests, the model first stored intact laps; plasticity was then frozen while a fixed fraction
of either the MEC-like or LEC-like input units was set to zero (Fig. 1E).


2.2   Readout compatibility constrains rapid associative storage

We first isolated the relationship between an instructed CA1 code and its fixed output decoder
(Fig. 2A). Twenty independently initialized autoencoders were pretrained on structured MEC+LEC
patterns and then frozen. Pattern order was shuffled, so this assay retained the marginal spatial–cue
structure without presenting a traversed track sequence. For each initialization, 28 held-out patterns
were stored once under five paired conditions. In the aligned condition, the IS remained in the
coordinates learned by the decoder. In the fixed-permutation condition, the same non-identity
permutation was applied to every IS while the decoder was left unchanged. In the matched-decoder
condition, the corresponding decoder columns were permuted together with the IS. Random-content
and no-plasticity conditions controlled for storing an incorrect association and for leaving the

Under the instructive-driven rule, aligned instructions produced a mean output–target cosine of
0.880 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.870–0.891), whereas a fixed coordinate permutation reduced it
to 0.247 (0.207–0.286). The paired aligned-minus-permuted difference was 0.634 (0.592–0.676). The
same coordinate dependence appeared under the error-driven rule: aligned and permuted means
were 0.872 (0.858–0.887) and 0.178 (0.147–0.209), respectively, giving a paired difference of 0.694
(0.658–0.731). Permuting the decoder together with the IS restored the aligned mean exactly for
each rule. Random-content recall remained lower (0.355 for the instructive-driven rule and 0.315
for the error-driven rule), as did the shared no-plasticity control (0.114). The matched-decoder
restoration is an algebraic coordinate control: the permutation preserves the IS values but changes
their meaning to the unchanged decoder. Reconstruction therefore depends on compatibility between
the instructed CA1 coordinates and the fixed output basis, not on information content alone. Under
this reference configuration, the two rules reached similar aligned one-shot recall despite using
different update signals: the instructive-driven rule performs a target-gated associative update,
whereas the error-driven rule makes a bounded residual correction.


2.3   Cue exchanges induce reproducible CA1 reconfiguration

We next exposed the model to 40 track laps in which two cue identities exchanged positions every 10
laps (Fig. 2B). A paired no-swap control used the same root seeds, pretrained encoder and decoder,
MEC-like trajectories, lap count, plasticity parameters, and exact EC–CA3 projection, but retained
the initial cue assignment throughout. After every training lap, plasticity was paused and the model
was probed in the context scheduled for that lap.
For the instructive-driven rule, mean unit-wise tuning similarity between consecutive probes fell to
0.365 at cue exchanges (95% CI, 0.348–0.382), compared with 0.845 at the same lap boundaries in
the no-swap control (0.832–0.857). The paired swap-minus-control difference was −0.479 (−0.498
to −0.461). Under the error-driven rule, exchange-boundary similarity was 0.451 (0.436–0.466),
compared with 0.827 in the matched control (0.817–0.838), for a paired difference of −0.376 (−0.396
to −0.356). At non-exchange transitions in the alternating schedule, similarity remained high for
both the instructive-driven rule (0.833, 0.823–0.843) and the error-driven rule (0.821, 0.814–0.828).
The abrupt tuning changes were therefore associated with the altered cue sequence rather than with
continued learning alone.
In a separate realization of the same protocol, we aligned the three exchange events to their
first post-exchange lap (Fig. 2E). Under the instructive-driven rule, consecutive-lap similarity at
the exchange was 0.375 (95% CI, 0.351–0.398), versus 0.851 (0.839–0.863) at matched no-swap
boundaries; the paired difference was −0.477 (−0.499 to −0.454). Under the error-driven rule, the
corresponding values were 0.462 (0.443–0.480) and 0.833 (0.822–0.845), with a paired difference of
−0.372 (−0.391 to −0.352). By the following lap, similarity recovered to 0.769 (0.756–0.783) and
0.819 (0.806–0.832), respectively. Thus the response was concentrated at the cue exchange rather
than developing as nonspecific drift over the subsequent block.
Because the probe context also changes at an exchange, this discontinuity does not by itself
distinguish synaptic updating from the immediate response to a different retrieval cue.
The lap-by-lap population representational-similarity matrix provided a complementary view
(Fig. 2F). Within-block maps remained similar, exchanges separated the sequence into distinct

blocks, and nonadjacent blocks carrying the same cue assignment resembled one another. The model
therefore alternated between cue-dependent population states rather than accumulating a wholly
novel map after every block. Retrospective comparison with the final map of each new block further
showed that the first post-exchange representation was already closer to the eventual adapted map
than to the pre-exchange map, followed by smaller within-block changes (Supplementary Fig. S2A,B).
Endpoint similarities were broadly distributed across CA1 units, demonstrating that the population
effect was not produced by a single uniform response class (Supplementary Fig. S2C,D).
After learning, plasticity was frozen and both cue arrangements were probed. The representative
error-driven population contained spatially stable fields, amplitude-modulated responses, and fields
whose preferred position changed with cue identity (Fig. 2C). Across seeds, mean unit-wise, mean-
centered spatial stability was 0.330 (95% CI, 0.297–0.363) under the instructive-driven rule and 0.464
(0.441–0.486) under the error-driven rule. Mean absolute cue modulation was 0.132 (0.130–0.135)
and 0.124 (0.120–0.128), respectively. In both rules, the unit-level distributions were continuous
rather than separated into discrete response classes (Fig. 2D), and strong cue modulation was
concentrated among units with low or negative cross-context stability. These distributions are
descriptive: units nested within a seed were not treated as independent replicates, and the analysis
is not a formal classification of rate versus global remapping. Operationally, changing the cue
arrangement changed both the EC-derived instruction and the CA3-like key, producing a mixture
of stable and context-dependent recalled CA1 activity.


2.4   Selective EC degradation dissociates cue and spatial readout

We then asked whether the stored association remained usable when one EC input modality was
selectively corrupted (Fig. 3). Each model stored 20 intact laps with a fixed cue arrangement.
Plasticity was frozen, and independent masks requested removal of 0, 25, 50, 75, 90, or 100% of
eligible units. Because each modality contained 25 units, the implemented fractions were 0, 24, 48,
76, 88, and 100%, respectively. Each fraction used 12 masks per seed and 20 paired root seeds; the
numerical summary below focuses on 22-of-25 (88%) partial degradation. The principal control
replaced the normal sparse EC–CA3 map with a dense common map in which every CA3-like unit
received the same average EC input. This manipulation removes item-specific key structure and is
therefore a degenerate-key, not a sparsity-matched, control.
Under LEC-like degradation, cue identity accuracy for the normal key declined from 1.000 to 0.871 at
88% dropout under the instructive-driven rule (95% CI at 88%, 0.803–0.938) and from 1.000 to 0.829
under the error-driven rule (0.766–0.893; Fig. 3A). The corresponding 88%-dropout changes were
−0.129 (−0.197 to −0.062) and −0.171 (−0.234 to −0.107). MEC output–target cosine remained
near its instructive-driven baseline (0.736 at baseline and 0.723 at 88% dropout; change −0.013,
−0.029 to 0.003), whereas it declined from 0.904 to 0.780 under the error-driven rule (change −0.125,
−0.136 to −0.113; Fig. 3B). Thus spatial output remained substantially readable under LEC-like
corruption, although it was not strictly invariant under the error-driven rule.
MEC-like degradation produced the complementary loss of position information. Nearest-position
accuracy declined from 0.163 to 0.070 under the instructive-driven rule (88%-dropout change
−0.093, 95% CI −0.106 to −0.079) and from 0.666 to 0.069 under the error-driven rule (change
−0.597, −0.636 to −0.557; chance = 0.02; Fig. 3C). Cue identity remained 1.000 for every seed
and degradation level under both rules (Fig. 3D). The low baseline position accuracy under the
instructive-driven rule is itself informative: repeated error-driven correction yielded a substantially

2.5    Parameter optimization identifies rule-specific operating regimes and load-
       dependent trade-offs

The matched reference configuration above isolates consequences of changing the update equation,
but it need not be favorable to both rules. We therefore performed separate covariance matrix
adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES) searches for the instructive-driven and error-driven rules on
three fixed development seeds. Within each generated MEC+LEC lap, storage samples were shuffled
before learning, preserving the input distribution while removing adjacent track states. The objective
equally weighted clean recall, recall after 21-of-25 (84%) MEC-like degradation, and recall after 21-


                                                     8
of-25 (84%) LEC-like degradation (nominal requested fraction, 85%); these development evaluations
were not treated as experimental replicates. To keep the representational basis common across the
principal and optimization-informed analyses, these searches fixed KCA1 = 5 and both autoencoder
gains at 25. The independently selected medial-temporal-lobe (MTL) model configurations used
fan-in 36, KCA3 = 6, βCA3 = 286.747, βCA1 = 9.160, and α = 0.03366 for the instructive-driven rule,
versus fan-in 9, KCA3 = 5, βCA3 = 54.400, βCA1 = 26.928, and α = 0.08197 for the error-driven rule.
Their development-set composite scores were 0.714 and 0.818, respectively. Because both parameter
vectors were selected on the same three seeds used to define the objective, these values describe
optimization outcomes rather than unbiased estimates of generalization.
A one-parameter-at-a-time analysis evaluated multipliers from 0.1 to 9 around each selected vector
while fixing all other parameters (Fig. 4A). Ten independent evaluation seeds, disjoint from the
search seeds, supplied paired score differences and their across-seed standard deviations. The
composite score changed smoothly near most selected values but was not uniformly insensitive. The
largest mean decreases were produced by applying the largest KCA3 multiplier to instructive-driven
plasticity (−0.231) and reducing βCA3 tenfold for error-driven plasticity (−0.477). The requested
ninefold increase in KCA3 was clipped from 54 to the allowed maximum of 49. Small positive mean
differences at a few tested points were at most 0.002 and were small relative to their across-seed
variation. The analysis therefore supports a local operating region while revealing important
parameter asymmetries; it is not a global robustness guarantee.
We next tested the selected configurations on 10 held-out seeds while increasing associative load
from 1 to all 90 non-repeating ordered pairs drawn from 10 cue identities (Fig. 4B). Every pair was
presented three times, and exact two-cue recall was measured from clean probes and after 12-of-25
(48%) LEC-unit dropout. Instructive-driven clean recall declined from 1.000 ± 0.000 at one pair to
0.210 ± 0.043 at 10 pairs and 0.138 ± 0.016 at 90 pairs (mean ± SEM); corresponding degraded
recall was 1.000 ± 0.000, 0.035 ± 0.012, and 0.051 ± 0.006. Error-driven clean recall was 1.000 ± 0.000,
0.390±0.046, and 0.151±0.022 at the same loads, with degraded values of 0.775±0.095, 0.098±0.024,
and 0.046 ± 0.003. Thus both selected regimes exhibited interference as load increased, with an
error-driven advantage at intermediate clean load but convergence toward low exact-pair recall at
the largest load. These curves are empirical load profiles for this cue vocabulary, training schedule,
and model size rather than universal capacity estimates.

3    Discussion

This study isolates a constraint on rapid associative learning: information stored in a population
state must remain accessible to the circuit that reads it. A fixed permutation preserved the values
of the instructed CA1 vector but impaired reconstruction by an unchanged decoder. Transforming
the decoder with the same permutation restored performance. This rescue follows from the model’s
coordinate symmetry, rather than providing an independent empirical discovery. Its value is to
separate an incompatible readout from degradation of the target representation. The additional
track, degradation, and load assays show how associative learning operates within the compatible
basis, and where its performance fails.
The contribution is therefore not the introduction of structured hippocampal representations or of a
learning role for CA1. Earlier models assign the direct EC–CA1 pathway a role in learning shared
statistical structure [22], and scaffold-based theories use pre-existing retrieval states to organize
new associations [2]. Our model makes a related but narrower distinction between retrieval-key
construction, CA3–CA1 association, and output decoding. It removes recurrent retrieval dynamics
to expose the relation between the latter two operations. This simplification allows a controlled
compatibility assay, but precludes claims that the architecture accounts for the broader episodic or
spatial capacities of those models.
Compatibility also differs from representational invariance. A code need not remain unchanged if
its variations preserve the output required by a task, or if downstream weights adapt appropriately.
Motor-cortical studies demonstrate constraints imposed by an existing activity repertoire [6, 21];
stable latent dynamics further distinguish population-level structure from fixed decoding of individual
recorded neurons [5]. Models of self-healing readouts offer a complementary solution in which
downstream plasticity tracks changes in the input code [20]. Here the decoder is deliberately frozen.
Accordingly, the simulations establish the cost of a mismatch under a fixed readout, not the necessity
of preserving CA1 coordinates when recipient circuits are free to adapt. Long-term stability and
representational drift remain outside the tested regime.
The two plasticity rules implement different computational uses of an instruction. The instructive-
driven rule updates an association without comparing the target with current recall; the error-driven
rule corrects under- and overexpressed CA1 components through bounded synaptic changes. Both
yielded similar aligned one-shot reconstruction under the reference configuration, but repeated
error-driven learning produced higher clean position accuracy and greater cross-context spatial
stability. These results show that matching the target basis does not guarantee identical learning
dynamics or equally discriminable outputs. Although motivated by rapid instructed plasticity
[1, 17, 25], neither rule implements a temporal eligibility trace or a dendritic plateau. In particular,
the signed target–recall discrepancy in the error-driven rule is a computational assumption, not an
experimentally identified CA1 error signal. Evidence for entorhinal teaching signals and nonspatial
BTSP-like field formation supports the motif, but not this exact implementation [4, 7].
The cue-track results reveal reproducible context dependence rather than unconstrained creation
of a new map at each transition. Population maps were similar within a cue assignment and
when that assignment recurred. Consecutive-lap tuning similarity fell at exchanges and recovered
on the following lap, while individual units showed continuous mixtures of spatial stability and
cue modulation. These features are qualitatively related to cue-sensitive changes in hippocampal
firing [12, 18] and to learning-related CA1 field reorganization [7, 14, 24]. However, the transition
assay changes both the training context and the context used for the recall probe. The boundary


                                                  11
discontinuity therefore combines changes in the learned weights with the immediate effect of
presenting a different retrieval key. Pausing plasticity during each probe prevents further learning
during measurement; it does not isolate the contribution of plasticity to the transition. Fixed-
context probes across training, together with context switches at fixed weights, would be needed to
distinguish learning-driven reorganization from cue-dependent re-expression. Nor were the simulated
unit distributions fitted to biological field statistics or used to classify rate and global remapping.
The degradation experiments likewise support a specific interpretation. Partial input loss left
useful output information under the normal key map, and corruption of one input partition
preferentially affected its associated readout. Yet there is no recurrent CA3 network or attractor
settling: robustness arises from a distributed feedforward input-to-key transformation and the
learned key-to-content mapping. Degraded-input reconstruction alone cannot identify a recurrent
completion mechanism [13, 19]. The dense common-key control makes every CA3-like unit receive
the same EC average and eliminates item-specific keys. Its failure establishes the importance of
distinguishable keys in this circuit, not a unique advantage of sparse connectivity. Sparsity-matched
and dimensionality-matched controls would be required for that stronger claim.
The constructed MEC/LEC partition also limits anatomical interpretation. Each input half has
a designated information type and corresponding readout, so part of the observed dissociation is
expected by design. Biological medial and lateral EC contain mixed signals, and both can contribute
to nonspatial CA1 learning [3, 4, 10]. Moreover, the retrieval query and reconstruction target are
derived from the same EC pattern. Removing input units tests recovery of missing components of
a previously experienced pattern; it does not test retrieval of an independently specified episode
feature absent from the query. Such a heteroassociative task would provide a stronger test of
cue-to-content retrieval.
The optimization-informed analyses extend, but do not replace, the matched rule comparison.
Separate searches selected markedly different fan-in, sparsity, gain, and learning-rate combinations.
Their sensitivity and load curves therefore compare complete operating regimes, not update equations
in isolation. Held-out perturbations indicate that useful performance extends beyond one exact
parameter vector, while large changes can be detrimental. One-at-a-time perturbations do not
establish global robustness or identify unique biological parameters. Similarly, the 90-pair load
assay measures an empirical interference profile. The autoencoder already represents the full cue
vocabulary, total training increases with load, and the 10-cue templates are sparser than those in
the two-cue assay. Exact-pair reconstruction should not be interpreted as evidence for learning
temporal associations between the cues or as a universal storage-capacity estimate.
Several tests follow directly from these limitations. First, varying target coordinates and decoder
adaptation independently could compare preservation of a fixed readout with compensatory down-
stream learning. Second, crossing probe context with learning history would separate immediate
key-dependent responses from lasting changes in CA1 weights. Third, load experiments that hold
total writes, cue coverage, and template sparsity fixed would clarify the sources of interference.
More broadly, biological tests should compare the behavioral consequences of CA1 changes with
their effects on recipient populations, rather than treating the magnitude of CA1 change alone as a
measure of memory disruption.
Within these bounds, the model provides a compact demonstration of how a previously established
representational basis can support rapid associative storage. Its central implication is relational: the
usefulness of a retrieved CA1 state depends on the mapping that interprets it. Readout compatibility
is thus a testable constraint on models of hippocampal learning, while its biological implementation


                                                  12
and long-term maintenance remain open questions.


4     Methods

4.1    Implementation, reproducibility, and statistical treatment

Simulations were implemented in Python using NumPy and PyTorch and run on CPU. Figures 2 and
3A–D used 20 root seeds (51001–51020); sensitivity used 10 independent root seeds (54001–54010),
and the cue-pair load experiment used 10 held-out root seeds (53001–53010). Autoencoder and
MTL hyperparameter searches used separate fixed development seeds (41001–41003 and 42001–
42003, respectively). Conditions and plasticity rules within an experiment were paired by root seed
whenever their parameterization permitted a paired comparison. The experiment scripts write a
resolved JSON configuration, compressed NumPy arrays, and a tidy CSV source-data table; plotting
functions generate the individual panels from those saved arrays. The composed vector figures do
not alter the underlying values.
For the evolution-informed analyses, bounded hyperparameter configurations were selected using the
covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES), a stochastic derivative-free optimizer
[8]. A standalone autoencoder search minimized held-out reconstruction error. Independent MTL
searches maximized a composite of clean and selectively degraded recall for each plasticity rule
while retaining the common reference autoencoder basis; the two searches therefore report related
but non-composed engineering analyses. Search data, objectives, bounds, and selected values are
reported in Appendix A. The shared reference configuration was retained separately to provide a
matched update-rule comparison and was not produced by the rule-specific searches. CMA-ES is an
outer-loop engineering procedure, distinct from Adam-based autoencoder pretraining and from the
local CA3–CA1 updates studied here. Optimization generations and development seeds were not
counted as experimental replicates.
The unit of computational replication was the root seed, not an individual memory, CA1 unit, track
position, or corruption mask. Values were first averaged over observations nested within a seed and
then summarized across seeds. For a seed-level statistic ys , s = 1, . . . , S, curves and error bars show

4.4   Paired plasticity-rule design

Both rules were evaluated in every main experiment. In the matched reference experiments (Figs. 2
and 3A–D), they shared EC inputs, pretrained autoencoder weights, memory hyperparameters,
CA3 wiring seeds, storage order, and–in the degradation experiment–corruption masks. Each
rule had its own CA3–CA1 weight matrix, which changed according to the corresponding update.
These comparisons therefore isolate the update equation within one common operating regime.
Supplementary Fig. S1 collects four paired contrasts derived directly from those result arrays; it is
not a separate simulation.
The optimization, sensitivity, and cue-pair load analyses answer a different question: how each
rule behaves near a separately selected operating point. Those analyses shared data-generation
procedures and random-seed streams but used rule-specific CA3 fan-in, sparsity, gains, and learning
rates. Their between-rule differences therefore reflect complete operating regimes and must not be
attributed to the update equation alone.


4.5   Readout-compatibility experiment

For each seed, the 28 held-out EC patterns were assigned CA3-like retrieval keys and pretrained
CA1 instructions, then written once in a shared random order. The aligned condition used the
original IS and decoder. The fixed-permutation condition applied one seed-specific CA1 permutation
to every IS while leaving the decoder unchanged. The matched-decoder condition used the same
permuted instructions and correspondingly permuted decoder columns. The random-content
condition associated each key with a shuffled memory instruction. The no-plasticity condition
retained zero CA3–CA1 weights. Recall was evaluated using cosine similarity between reconstructed
EC output and the corresponding EC target. Each seed’s plotted value is its mean across 28
memories.

4.7    Selective degradation experiments

Models stored 20 intact laps with cue assignment [0, 1]. During frozen recall, a fixed random subset

integer, the corresponding numbers removed were 0, 6, 12, 19, 22, and 25 units (effective fractions

for reference. LEC-like degradation operated on the final 25 input units; MEC-like degradation
operated on the first 25.

Appendix A: Evolutionary hyperparameter selection

Optimization strategy

Hyperparameters were selected with the covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES),
a stochastic, derivative-free optimizer for nonlinear and non-convex objectives [8]. CMA-ES sampled
a multivariate normal population in standardized coordinates and adapted its mean, covariance,
and global step size from candidate rankings. If g is a standardized genome, model parameters were
decoded as
                                       θ(g) = clip[ℓ,u] (c + s ⊙ g),                           (31)
where c, s, ℓ, and u are the parameter-wise centers, scales, lower bounds, and upper bounds.
Count-valued parameters were rounded to the nearest integer after clipping. A quadratic penalty
discouraged samples outside the standardized bounds before clipping. Candidate evaluations were
parallelized across CPU processes, but parallelization did not change the objective.
The search was used only for engineering model selection. Model weights were not encoded in the
genome: autoencoder weights were still learned with Adam, and CA3–CA1 weights were still changed
by the local plasticity rule. Fixed development seeds made candidate evaluations deterministic and
comparable. No seed was optimized separately, and development seeds were disjoint from the 20
seeds used for the matched principal experiments, the 10 independent seeds used for sensitivity
analysis, and the 10 seeds used for the cue-pair load test. A single CMA-ES run was performed
for each reported search, so the results characterize the selected run rather than variability across
independent optimizer restarts.


Autoencoder objective

The autoencoder search varied KCA1 , the latent sparse-activation gain βlatent , and the decoder
gain βout . It used three development seeds (41001–41003). For each candidate and seed, separate
autoencoders were trained for 64 epochs on (i) 1,024 sparse binary EC patterns and (ii) 20 MEC+LEC
track laps, then tested on 128 disjoint sparse patterns and five independently generated track laps.
The minimized objective was
